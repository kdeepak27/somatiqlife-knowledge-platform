## Definition
Added sugar refers to sugars and syrups incorporated into foods and beverages during processing or preparation, as opposed to naturally occurring sugars found intrinsically in whole foods like fruits and dairy.

## Why This Matters
Understanding how much added sugar constitutes 'too much' is vital for preventing nutrition-related health problems, including obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. Clear, evidence-based guidance helps consumers make informed dietary choices.

## What Is Added Sugar and How Is It Defined?
Added sugar includes all sugars added to foods and drinks during processing or preparation, such as table sugar (sucrose), high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), honey, and fruit juice concentrates. It excludes naturally occurring sugars found in whole fruits, vegetables, and milk. Terms like "free sugars" used by some health organizations can overlap with added sugars but also include sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and fruit juices.

## Differences Between Added Sugar and Natural Sugar
Natural sugars exist intrinsically in foods like fruits (fructose) and dairy (lactose). Added sugars are those introduced externally to increase sweetness, improve texture, or preserve food. Unlike natural sugars, added sugars contribute calories with little or no essential nutrients (empty calories). This distinction is important because added sugars contribute to health risks without nutritional benefit.

## How Does the Body Process Added Sugar?
When consumed, added sugars are broken down mainly into glucose and fructose. Glucose is metabolized by most body cells for energy, whereas fructose is metabolized primarily in the liver. Excessive fructose intake from added sugars can promote fat synthesis in the liver, contribute to insulin resistance, and increase blood triglycerides, which are risk factors for metabolic diseases.

## Recommended Limits on Added Sugar Intake
The World Health Organization recommends that added sugars make up less than 10% of total daily energy intake, with a conditional recommendation to further reduce to below 5% for added health benefits. For an average adult consuming 2000 kcal/day, 10% corresponds to about 50 grams (approximately 12 teaspoons) of added sugar daily. Many countries provide similar guidelines, highlighting that lower intake is beneficial.

## Health Risks Associated with Excessive Added Sugar
High added sugar intake is linked to increased risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dental caries, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. These risks stem from excessive calorie consumption, metabolic disturbances, and inflammatory processes triggered by sugar. The link is stronger with sugar-sweetened beverages due to rapid absorption and high sugar content.

## Common Dietary Sources of Added Sugar
Major sources include sugar-sweetened beverages (sodas, energy drinks), baked goods, candies, sweetened dairy products (ice cream, flavored yogurts), cereals, condiments (ketchup, sauces), and processed snacks. Many packaged foods contain added sugar under various names such as glucose, dextrose, maltose, and syrups.

## How to Identify Added Sugar on Nutrition Labels
Nutrition labels often list added sugars separately under sugars or contain ingredient lists with common added sugar names. The US FDA requires added sugars to be listed on the Nutrition Facts label, aiding consumers in quantifying intake. Understanding different sugar synonyms helps identify added sugars (e.g., sucrose, corn syrup, malt syrup).

## Limitations and Challenges in Measuring Added Sugar Intake
Estimating added sugar intake is complicated by inconsistent labeling regulations worldwide and presence of multiple sugar sources in foods. Some natural sugars resemble added sugars chemically, complicating analysis. Self-report dietary assessments are prone to under- or over-reporting sugar consumption.

## Misconceptions About Added Sugar
Common misconceptions include beliefs that natural sugars in fruit juices are harmless or that all sugars have the same metabolic effect. Another is the idea that sugar substitutes are equally harmful or completely safe—evidence varies. Clarifying these misconceptions is necessary for effective dietary advice.

## Practical Guidance to Manage Added Sugar Intake
Practical steps include reading food labels carefully, reducing consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, choosing whole fruits instead of fruit juices, cooking meals from fresh ingredients, and being aware of hidden sugars in processed foods. Gradual reduction helps adaptation and decreases cravings.

## Special Considerations for Children and Adolescents
Children and adolescents are vulnerable to high added sugar intake due to preferences for sweet foods and drinks. Excess sugar intake in youth is linked to increased risk of obesity and dental problems. Guidelines recommend even lower added sugar limits for children to support healthy growth and development.

## Safety Notes and When to Consult Health Professionals
While moderate added sugar consumption is generally safe within balanced diets, excessive intake can exacerbate existing metabolic conditions. Individuals with diabetes, obesity, or cardiovascular disease should consult healthcare providers for personalized guidance. Sudden drastic sugar restriction should be medically supervised in some cases.

## Practical Guidance
- Monitor and limit added sugar to less than 10% of daily calories, aiming for under 5% if possible.
- Read nutrition labels to identify sources of added sugar and total intake.
- Reduce or eliminate sugar-sweetened beverages from the diet.
- Choose whole fruits instead of fruit juices or concentrates.
- Prepare meals at home to control added sugar content.
- Be wary of hidden sugars in processed foods by checking ingredient lists.
- Gradually decrease sugar in recipes and beverages to adjust taste preferences.
- Educate children about the health effects of added sugar and encourage healthy eating habits.

## Safety Notes
- Consuming low to moderate amounts of added sugar is generally safe for healthy individuals.
- Excessive added sugar intake is linked to metabolic diseases and dental caries.
- People with chronic diseases should seek professional advice on sugar intake.
- Rapid or extreme sugar restriction may cause withdrawal-like symptoms; gradual reduction is recommended.
- Label reading is essential to avoid unintentional high sugar consumption.

## Common Misconceptions
### Natural fruit juices are just as healthy as whole fruits because they contain natural sugars.
**Reality:** Fruit juices often contain free sugars that count as added sugars and lack the fiber and nutrients found in whole fruits.

### All sugars, added or natural, have the same effect on the body.
**Reality:** Added sugars, especially in liquid form, can promote rapid spikes in blood sugar and have different metabolic impacts compared to sugars in whole foods.

### Sugar substitutes are equally harmful or have no benefits.
**Reality:** Evidence on sugar substitutes is mixed; some may help reduce calorie intake when used appropriately.

## Glossary
- **Added Sugar:** Sugars and syrups added to foods during processing or preparation, excluding naturally occurring sugars in whole foods.
- **Natural Sugar:** Sugars inherently present in whole fruits, vegetables, and dairy products, such as fructose and lactose.
- **Sucrose:** Common table sugar composed of glucose and fructose molecules.
- **High-Fructose Corn Syrup (HFCS):** A sweetener made from corn starch containing varying amounts of fructose and glucose used in many processed foods.
- **Empty Calories:** Calories from foods and drinks that provide energy but little or no nutritional value.
- **Free Sugars:** Sugars added to foods plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and fruit juices, as defined by WHO.
- **Insulin Resistance:** A condition where body cells do not respond properly to insulin, leading to elevated blood sugar levels.
- **Sugar-Sweetened Beverages (SSBs):** Drinks with added sugars such as sodas, energy drinks, and sweetened fruit drinks.
