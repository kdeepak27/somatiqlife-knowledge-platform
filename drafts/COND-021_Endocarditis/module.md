Safety considerations underscore careful antibiotic use and monitoring for adverse effects. Endocarditis differs from pericarditis and myocarditis despite overlapping cardiac involvement. ## Definition
Endocarditis is an inflammation of the endocardium, the inner lining of the heart chambers and valves. Most cases are infective endocarditis (IE), caused by microbial infection of heart structures, notably cardiac valves, resulting in vegetations and tissue damage.

## Why This Matters
Endocarditis is a potentially life-threatening disease that affects heart function and can cause systemic complications. Early recognition, diagnosis, and management are crucial to prevent severe outcomes such as heart failure, embolism, and death. Understanding endocarditis empowers patients and the public to recognize symptoms, prevent infection, and seek timely medical care.

## Definition and Types of Endocarditis
Endocarditis refers to inflammation of the endocardium, primarily affecting the heart valves. There are two principal types: infective endocarditis (IE), caused by bacteria, fungi, or other microorganisms; and non-infective endocarditis, such as marantic or Libman-Sacks endocarditis which is associated with autoimmune or thrombotic conditions. This section focuses mainly on infective endocarditis, the most common and clinically significant form.

## Pathophysiology and Mechanisms
In infective endocarditis, damaged endocardial surfaces become colonized by circulating pathogens, often following bacteremia. Microbes adhere to the exposed collagen or damaged valves, triggering accumulation of fibrin and platelets leading to formation of vegetations. These vegetations can cause local tissue destruction, valve dysfunction, and can embolize to distant organs, causing infarcts or metastatic infection. The immune response and microbial factors contribute to pathology.

## Common Causative Organisms
The most frequent pathogens in infective endocarditis include Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus viridans group, and Enterococcus species. Less commonly, fungi like Candida species or HACEK organisms (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella) can be responsible, especially in immunocompromised hosts or those with prosthetic valves.

## Risk Factors for Endocarditis
Risk factors include pre-existing heart valve abnormalities (e.g., rheumatic heart disease, mitral valve prolapse), prosthetic heart valves, previous endocarditis history, intravenous drug use, indwelling vascular catheters, and invasive dental or surgical procedures. Advanced age and immunosuppression also increase risk. Identifying risk factors aids in prevention and early diagnosis.

## Signs and Symptoms of Endocarditis
Symptoms are often nonspecific and insidious. Common clinical features include persistent fever, chills, night sweats, fatigue, weight loss, new or changed heart murmurs, petechiae, splinter hemorrhages under nails, Osler nodes (painful nodules on fingers/toes), and Janeway lesions (painless hemorrhagic lesions on palms/soles). Symptoms may evolve over days to weeks.

## Diagnostic Evaluation
Diagnosis involves clinical assessment supported by microbiological and imaging studies. Blood cultures to isolate causative organisms are critical, ideally from multiple draws. Echocardiography (transthoracic or transesophageal) detects vegetations, abscesses, or valve dysfunction. The modified Duke Criteria are used clinically to integrate findings and guide diagnosis.

## Treatment and Management
Treatment requires prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy tailored to the identified pathogen and susceptibility profile, typically lasting four to six weeks. Surgery may be necessary in cases of valve destruction, heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or embolic complications. Multidisciplinary care including infectious disease and cardiology specialists improves outcomes.

## Prevention and Lifestyle Considerations
Preventive strategies include good dental hygiene, prophylactic antibiotics in select high-risk patients undergoing invasive procedures, and harm reduction (e.g., avoiding intravenous drug use). Lifestyle modifications include managing underlying cardiac conditions and promptly treating infections. Patient education is essential for prevention and early recognition.

## Complications and Prognosis
Complications include heart failure from valve incompetence, systemic emboli causing stroke or infarctions in organs, abscess formation, and persistent bacteremia. Prognosis depends on timely diagnosis and effective treatment. Delays increase morbidity and mortality. Ongoing surveillance and follow-up are critical after treatment.

## Limitations and Emerging Evidence
Challenges include diagnostic delays due to nonspecific symptoms, difficulty in culturing fastidious organisms, and rising antibiotic resistance. Research is ongoing into rapid molecular diagnostics, novel antimicrobial agents, and improved surgical techniques. Awareness of limitations and evolving evidence is important for optimizing patient care.

## Safety and Monitoring During Treatment
Monitoring for antibiotic side effects such as nephrotoxicity or allergic reactions is necessary. Surgical interventions carry risks including bleeding or arrhythmias. Regular cardiac assessment and laboratory tests help detect complications early. Patient adherence and communication with healthcare teams enhance safety and outcomes.

## Practical Guidance
- Seek medical evaluation if persistent fever or unexplained illness occurs, especially with heart conditions.
- Maintain good oral hygiene and regular dental checkups to reduce risk.
- Inform healthcare providers about any history of heart valve disease before invasive procedures.
- Avoid intravenous drug use and practice safe injection techniques to prevent infection.
- Adhere strictly to prescribed antibiotic regimens if diagnosed with endocarditis.
- Report any new or changing symptoms such as heart murmurs or skin lesions promptly.
- Discuss with your provider whether antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended for your specific risk profile.
- Understand that endocarditis treatment often requires several weeks of intravenous antibiotics in a hospital or specialized care.
- Keep follow-up appointments to monitor heart function and detect complications early.
- Educate caregivers and patients about signs of recurrence or complications.

## Safety Notes
- Do not self-medicate with antibiotics; misuse can worsen resistance and delay proper treatment.
- Inform your healthcare team of all medications and allergies before starting treatment.
- Monitor kidney and liver function during prolonged antibiotic therapy.
- Report signs of allergic reactions, such as rash or difficulty breathing, immediately.
- Avoid activities or behaviors that might expose you to infections if you have heart valve abnormalities.
- Post-surgical patients should follow all recovery instructions to avoid complications.

## Common Misconceptions
### Endocarditis only affects people with obvious heart disease.
**Reality:** Endocarditis can affect individuals with subtle or undiagnosed heart abnormalities and even healthy individuals under certain conditions, such as intravenous drug use.

### Endocarditis is contagious between people.
**Reality:** Endocarditis is not contagious; it results from infection of heart tissues typically following bacteremia, not person-to-person transmission.

### Antibiotics can cure endocarditis quickly in a few days.
**Reality:** Effective treatment requires prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy, often lasting four to six weeks or more.

### A heart murmur always indicates endocarditis.
**Reality:** Heart murmurs can occur for many reasons; a new or changed murmur in the context of infection may raise concern for endocarditis but is not diagnostic alone.

## Clinical Pearls
- Persistent fever with heart murmur should prompt consideration of endocarditis.
- Multiple blood cultures increase the likelihood of pathogen detection.
- Transesophageal echocardiography is more sensitive than transthoracic for detecting vegetations.
- Early surgical consultation is essential for patients with complications or prosthetic valves.
- Antibiotic stewardship helps prevent resistance and improves outcomes.

## Glossary
- **Endocardium:** The thin inner lining of the heart chambers and valves.
- **Vegetation:** An abnormal growth on heart valves composed of microorganisms, fibrin, and platelets, characteristic of endocarditis.
- **Bacteremia:** The presence of bacteria in the bloodstream.
- **Echocardiography:** A heart imaging technique using ultrasound to visualize heart structures and function.
- **Prosthetic Valve:** An artificial heart valve implanted to replace a damaged native valve.
- **Intravenous Drug Use (IVDU):** The practice of injecting drugs directly into veins, increasing infection risk.
- **Duke Criteria:** A set of clinical criteria used to diagnose infective endocarditis combining microbiology, imaging, and clinical findings.
- **Osler Nodes:** Tender, raised lesions on fingers or toes associated with endocarditis.
- **Janeway Lesions:** Painless, flat, red spots on palms or soles seen in endocarditis patients.
- **HACEK Organisms:** A group of fastidious Gram-negative bacteria that can cause endocarditis: Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, and Kingella species.
