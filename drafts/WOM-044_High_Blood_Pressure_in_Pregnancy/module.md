## Definition
High blood pressure in pregnancy refers to elevated arterial blood pressure levels detected in a woman during her pregnancy. It includes chronic hypertension existing before pregnancy, gestational hypertension developing after 20 weeks gestation without proteinuria, and preeclampsia characterized by hypertension with proteinuria and potential organ damage. These conditions represent a significant cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity globally.

## Why This Matters
Hypertension during pregnancy is a common complication that can seriously affect the health of both the mother and baby if left unmanaged. Understanding its types, causes, symptoms, and treatments is critical for ensuring safer pregnancies and reducing risks such as preterm birth, placental problems, and long-term cardiovascular consequences for mothers.

## Types of High Blood Pressure in Pregnancy
High blood pressure in pregnancy includes several distinct conditions: chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, and preeclampsia. Chronic hypertension exists before pregnancy or before 20 weeks gestation and persists afterward. Gestational hypertension is new onset elevated blood pressure diagnosed after 20 weeks without signs of preeclampsia such as proteinuria. Preeclampsia is hypertension after 20 weeks accompanied by proteinuria or evidence of organ damage, such as elevated liver enzymes or low platelet count. Each has implications for monitoring and management during pregnancy.

## Physiological Changes in Pregnancy Affecting Blood Pressure
Pregnancy induces dramatic cardiovascular adaptations including increased blood volume and cardiac output, as well as decreased systemic vascular resistance. Normally, these changes help meet fetal demands and generally cause blood pressure to decrease initially. However, abnormal vascular responses—such as impaired blood vessel dilation or oxidative stress—can cause blood pressure to rise abnormally in susceptible individuals, contributing to hypertensive disorders.

## Mechanisms Underlying Hypertension in Pregnancy
The development of high blood pressure disorders in pregnancy involves complex mechanisms such as abnormal placental development leading to poor blood flow (placental ischemia), endothelial dysfunction, immune and inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress. These processes trigger vasoconstriction and promote hypertension alongside systemic effects causing organ injury, particularly in preeclampsia.

## Risks and Complications of High Blood Pressure in Pregnancy
Uncontrolled hypertension during pregnancy is associated with serious risks including placental abruption (premature separation of the placenta), fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, and increased likelihood of cesarean delivery. Severe hypertension and preeclampsia can lead to maternal complications like stroke, seizures (eclampsia), liver and kidney damage, and increased long-term cardiovascular disease risk.

## Diagnostic Criteria and Evaluation
Diagnosis involves repeated blood pressure measurements showing systolic pressure of 140 mmHg or higher or diastolic pressure of 90 mmHg or higher on two occasions at least four hours apart after 20 weeks gestation. Urine tests assess proteinuria, often defined as ≥300 mg protein in 24-hour collection or a protein/creatinine ratio ≥0.3. Laboratory tests evaluate organ function and help differentiate preeclampsia from gestational hypertension.

## Management Strategies and Treatment Options
Treatment aims to control blood pressure to reduce maternal and fetal risks while avoiding harm from medications. Lifestyle interventions include moderate exercise and dietary modifications such as reduced salt intake. Pharmacologic treatments favored during pregnancy include labetalol, nifedipine, and methyldopa. Close monitoring guides therapy adjustments. Severe cases may require hospitalization and intravenous medication.

## Monitoring During Pregnancy and Postpartum
Frequent blood pressure checks and fetal growth assessments are crucial in managing hypertensive pregnant individuals. Postpartum monitoring is important as blood pressure can remain elevated or worsen after delivery. Early detection of complications enables timely intervention to minimize adverse outcomes.

## Prevention and Aspirin Use
Low-dose aspirin (60-150 mg daily) started during the late first trimester is recommended for pregnant individuals at high risk for preeclampsia based on clinical criteria or biomarkers. This intervention reduces the incidence and severity of preeclampsia by improving placental blood flow. Evidence supports aspirin as a safe preventive therapy when used appropriately.

## Safety Considerations and Medication Restrictions
Some antihypertensive medications such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and direct renin inhibitors are contraindicated in pregnancy due to teratogenic risks. Medications should only be initiated or continued under medical supervision. Patients should be educated about warning signs of severe hypertension requiring emergency care, including severe headaches, visual disturbances, and abdominal pain.

## Common Misconceptions about Hypertension in Pregnancy
Misunderstandings include beliefs that all high blood pressure during pregnancy requires immediate medication, or that hypertension will always harm the baby. Some think lifestyle changes are ineffective or fear all medications are unsafe. Clear evidence indicates individualized approaches are necessary, with some cases needing pharmacotherapy while others benefit from monitoring and non-pharmacologic measures.

## Long-term Health Implications for Mother and Child
Pregnancy-related hypertension, especially preeclampsia, is linked to increased risk of chronic hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and kidney disease later in life for the mother. Children born to affected pregnancies may have higher risks of metabolic and cardiovascular conditions. Awareness promotes ongoing health management postpartum.

## Practical Guidance
- Monitor blood pressure regularly during pregnancy and postpartum as recommended by healthcare providers.
- Adopt lifestyle modifications including balanced diet, moderate exercise, and stress reduction to help control blood pressure.
- Discuss all medication use with a healthcare provider to ensure safety for both mother and baby.
- Report any symptoms such as severe headaches, visual changes, or abdominal pain promptly, as these may indicate severe hypertension or preeclampsia.
- Follow prescribed treatment plans, including low-dose aspirin if recommended, to reduce risk of complications.
- Attend all prenatal visits for monitoring of maternal and fetal health.
- Understand that some forms of hypertension may require specialized care or early delivery to protect health.
- Avoid self-medicating or stopping prescribed antihypertensive medications without consulting a doctor.

## Safety Notes
- Do not use ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or direct renin inhibitors during pregnancy due to teratogenicity.
- Seek emergency medical attention if experiencing warning signs of severe preeclampsia such as severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling, or epigastric pain.
- Ensure blood pressure medications prescribed in pregnancy have established safety profiles.
- Inform your healthcare provider about all medications and supplements being taken.
- Do not delay prenatal care or blood pressure monitoring; early detection is vital for preventing complications.

## Common Misconceptions
### All high blood pressure during pregnancy requires immediate medication.
**Reality:** Not all cases require instant pharmacological treatment; mild gestational hypertension may be managed with monitoring and lifestyle changes.

### Medications for high blood pressure are unsafe in pregnancy.
**Reality:** Certain antihypertensive medications such as labetalol and nifedipine have well-established safety profiles and are commonly used during pregnancy.

### Hypertension in pregnancy always harms the baby.
**Reality:** With careful management, many pregnant individuals with hypertension have healthy pregnancies and babies.

## Glossary
- **Hypertension:** Abnormally high blood pressure, typically defined as systolic pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic pressure ≥90 mmHg.
- **Preeclampsia:** A pregnancy-specific hypertensive disorder characterized by high blood pressure and signs of organ damage, typically including proteinuria, occurring after 20 weeks gestation.
- **Gestational Hypertension:** High blood pressure that develops after 20 weeks of pregnancy without the presence of proteinuria or other organ dysfunction.
- **Proteinuria:** The presence of excess protein in the urine, often a marker of kidney involvement in preeclampsia.
- **Placental Ischemia:** Reduced blood flow to the placenta, often implicated in the pathogenesis of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy.
- **Endothelial Dysfunction:** Impaired function of the inner lining of blood vessels which contributes to the development of hypertension and organ damage.
- **Labetalol:** A beta-blocker medication commonly used to treat hypertension during pregnancy due to its safety profile.
- **Low-dose Aspirin:** A preventive treatment consisting of small doses of aspirin (typically 60-150 mg daily) to reduce the risk of preeclampsia in high-risk pregnancies.
