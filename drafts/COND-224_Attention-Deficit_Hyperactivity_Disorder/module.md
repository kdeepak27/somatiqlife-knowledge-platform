## Definition
Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by ongoing patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with functioning or development. It is one of the most frequent childhood psychiatric disorders, often persisting into adulthood and affecting social, academic, and occupational performance.

## Why This Matters
ADHD affects millions worldwide and can significantly impact quality of life if untreated. Early recognition and appropriate management improve outcomes, educational attainment, relationships, and overall well-being. Public understanding reduces stigma and promotes supportive environments for affected individuals.

## What Is ADHD?
ADHD is characterized by a consistent pattern of symptoms involving attention deficits, excessive activity levels, and impulsive behaviors beyond expected norms for the individual's developmental stage. These symptoms lead to difficulties in academic, social, and occupational settings. ADHD is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder to emphasize its onset during developmental years and its basis in brain differences affecting behavior control.

## Causes and Risk Factors
ADHD results from complex interactions between genetic, environmental, and neurological factors. Genetic studies show heritability estimates around 70-80%, indicating a strong familial component. Environmental exposures such as prenatal alcohol or tobacco use, premature birth, low birth weight, and early childhood adversity also contribute. Neuroimaging reveals altered brain structures and functions in frontal cortex and basal ganglia, areas involved in attention and impulse regulation.

## Types of ADHD
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5), identifies three primary presentations of ADHD: Predominantly Inattentive Type (characterized mainly by attention deficits), Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive Type (marked by hyperactivity and impulsivity), and Combined Type (when both symptom groups are significant). These types present with differing clinical features and may vary by age and individual.

## Symptoms and Clinical Presentation
Common symptoms include difficulty sustaining attention, forgetfulness, fidgeting, interrupting others, and excessive talking. Children may struggle with academic tasks, follow-through, and peer relationships, whereas adults may experience restlessness, organizational challenges, and difficulties managing time. Symptom expression often changes with age, with hyperactivity reducing but inattention persisting.

## Evaluation and Diagnosis
Diagnosis of ADHD is clinical, based on medical history, symptom assessment, and exclusion of other conditions. Standardized rating scales and input from multiple informants (parents, teachers) support the diagnosis. Diagnostic criteria per DSM-5 require symptom presence before age 12, occurrence in multiple settings, and significant impairment. Differential diagnoses include learning disabilities, anxiety disorders, and mood disorders.

## Neurobiology and Physiology
ADHD involves dysregulation of neurotransmitter systems including dopamine and norepinephrine, affecting neural circuits responsible for executive functions, attention control, and motor activity. Brain imaging studies demonstrate differences in volume and activity in regions such as the prefrontal cortex, caudate nucleus, and cerebellum. These neurobiological features underpin the behavioral symptoms observed.

## Management Strategies
Management of ADHD includes pharmacological treatments, primarily stimulant medications (e.g., methylphenidate, amphetamines), which enhance dopaminergic and noradrenergic activity improving attention and impulse control. Non-stimulant options (e.g., atomoxetine) are alternatives with different side effect profiles. Behavioral therapies, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy and parent training programs, support skill development and symptom coping. Multimodal approaches combining medication and psychosocial interventions are often most effective.

## Lifestyle and Environmental Considerations
Lifestyle modifications can complement medical treatment for ADHD. Structured routines, adequate sleep, regular physical activity, reduced screen time, and balanced nutrition may help reduce symptom severity. Educational accommodations and organizational tools enable better functioning. Environmental support from family, school, and workplace is critical to successful management.

## Common Misconceptions About ADHD
Misconceptions include beliefs that ADHD is caused by poor parenting, lack of discipline, or is simply a behavioral problem without biological basis. Another myth is that ADHD only affects children or that it can be cured by willpower. These inaccuracies hinder recognition and appropriate treatment. ADHD is a valid neurodevelopmental disorder with evidence-based interventions.

## Safety and Limitations of Treatments
Pharmacologic treatments for ADHD are generally safe when monitored, but side effects such as appetite loss, sleep disturbances, and cardiovascular concerns warrant attention. Stimulant misuse and potential dependency are considerations especially in adolescents and adults. Behavioral therapies have minimal adverse effects but require sustained commitment. Limitations include variable response rates and incomplete symptom resolution, emphasizing personalized treatment planning.

## Living With ADHD: Adult Considerations
ADHD persists into adulthood for many, sometimes presenting differently with dominant inattentiveness and executive dysfunction. Adults with ADHD may face challenges with employment, relationships, and mental health. Diagnosis in adults requires careful history-taking, and management may involve medication, coaching, and psychotherapy. Awareness improves coping and life outcomes.

## Practical Guidance
- Seek evaluation from qualified healthcare providers when ADHD is suspected.
- Adhere to prescribed treatments and communicate frequently with clinicians about symptom changes and side effects.
- Implement structured daily routines and use organizational aids to improve function.
- Engage in physical activity and ensure adequate sleep to support brain health.
- Educate family members and educators about ADHD to foster supportive environments.
- Monitor for co-occurring mental health conditions such as anxiety or depression.
- Consider multimodal treatment combining medication and behavioral interventions.
- Avoid reliance on myths; seek evidence-based information about ADHD.

## Safety Notes
- Inform healthcare providers about all medications and health conditions before starting ADHD treatment.
- Regularly monitor blood pressure and heart rate if taking stimulant medications.
- Report any adverse effects such as mood changes or sleep problems promptly to a clinician.
- Avoid abrupt discontinuation of ADHD medications without medical advice.
- Consider non-pharmacologic interventions particularly in young children or in cases of mild symptoms.

- Tailor treatment plans to individual needs respecting symptom profile and comorbid conditions.
- Monitor adherence and side effects regularly during pharmacologic treatment.
- Incorporate behavioral and environmental strategies alongside medication.
- Educate patients and families to improve treatment understanding and engagement.
- Recognize adult ADHD and address its unique challenges.
- Promote stigma reduction by sharing validated information about ADHD.

## Glossary
- **ADHD (Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder):** A neurodevelopmental disorder involving problems with attention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity.
- **Neurodevelopmental Disorder:** A group of disorders that affect the development of the nervous system leading to abnormal brain function.
- **Inattention:** Difficulty sustaining focus or being easily distracted.
- **Hyperactivity:** Excessive movement or restlessness beyond typical levels.
- **Impulsivity:** Acting hastily without considering consequences.
- **Executive Functions:** Cognitive processes including planning, working memory, inhibition, and flexible thinking.
- **Stimulant Medications:** Drugs that increase activity of neurotransmitters like dopamine and norepinephrine to improve attention.
- **DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition):** A manual published by the American Psychiatric Association used to diagnose mental disorders.
