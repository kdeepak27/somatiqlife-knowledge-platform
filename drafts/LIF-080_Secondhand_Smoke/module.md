## Definition
Secondhand smoke (SHS), also called environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) or passive smoking, is smoke inhaled involuntarily from tobacco products used by others. It consists mainly of sidestream smoke from the burning end of a cigarette or other tobacco product combined with exhaled mainstream smoke from smokers. This mixture contains thousands of chemicals, many harmful, toxic, and carcinogenic, that can adversely affect the health of non-smokers who breathe it in.

## Why This Matters
Secondhand smoke exposure is a significant public health issue worldwide because it increases the risk of disease and death in non-smokers, including children and adults. It contributes to preventable illnesses like lung cancer, heart disease, and respiratory problems. Understanding how to recognize, minimize, and avoid secondhand smoke protects individual health and supports community health initiatives for cleaner air and safer environments.

## What is Secondhand Smoke?
Secondhand smoke (SHS) refers to the combination of sidestream smoke released from the lit end of a tobacco product plus the mainstream smoke exhaled by smokers. It contains over 7,000 chemicals, hundreds of which are toxic and about 70 are known carcinogens. Terms like environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) and passive smoking are synonymous with secondhand smoke. Exposure occurs primarily through involuntary inhalation in indoor environments where smoking occurs, including homes, cars, workplaces, and public spaces where permitted.

## Mechanisms of Health Effects from Secondhand Smoke
The harmful health effects of secondhand smoke arise from inhalation of a complex mixture of chemicals that damage respiratory airways, blood vessels, and organs systemically. Toxins such as nicotine, carbon monoxide, formaldehyde, benzene, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons cause inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage. These processes promote disease development including atherosclerosis, impaired lung function, and cancer. Children are especially vulnerable as their developing lungs and immune systems are less able to counteract damage.

## Physiological Impact of Secondhand Smoke Exposure
When a non-smoker inhales secondhand smoke, toxic compounds enter the bloodstream through lung tissues causing endothelial dysfunction, increased platelet aggregation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Immediate effects can include irritation of eyes and airways, coughing, and reduced lung function. Chronic exposure leads to structural lung changes, increased risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma exacerbations, and heightened cardiovascular disease risk due to prolonged vascular injury.

## Who is Most at Risk?
Certain populations face heightened risks related to secondhand smoke, including children, pregnant women, the elderly, and individuals with preexisting respiratory or cardiovascular conditions. Children exposed to SHS are more likely to develop sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), asthma, bronchitis, pneumonia, and ear infections. Pregnant women exposed to SHS have increased risks of low birth weight and pregnancy complications. Elderly individuals may experience worsening of chronic health conditions.

## Evidence Linking Secondhand Smoke to Lung Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease
Numerous epidemiological studies link secondhand smoke exposure with increased lung cancer risk in non-smokers. The U.S. Surgeon General reports confirm a causal relationship. Moreover, SHS contributes to cardiovascular diseases by promoting blood vessel narrowing and clot formation, increasing rates of heart attacks and stroke in exposed populations. The evidence strength is high for these health outcomes based on large population studies and toxicological data.

## Is There a Safe Level of Exposure?
Current scientific consensus indicates that there is no safe level of exposure to secondhand smoke. Even brief exposure can cause harmful physiological changes such as vascular endothelial dysfunction and airway irritation. Chronic or repeated exposure substantially raises disease risk. This makes complete avoidance the best protective strategy for health.

## Reducing Exposure to Secondhand Smoke
The most effective method to reduce SHS exposure is establishing and enforcing smoke-free environments in homes, cars, workplaces, and public areas. Smoke-free laws and policies have been shown to significantly reduce hospitalizations for heart attacks and respiratory illnesses. Additional steps include not allowing smoking indoors, encouraging smokers to quit, and educating families about risks.

## Are Electronic Cigarettes a Source of Secondhand Smoke?
Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes or vaping devices) produce aerosol rather than traditional smoke. Though they generally emit fewer toxins than combusted tobacco, secondhand exposure to e-cigarette aerosol is not harmless. Some studies show presence of nicotine, ultrafine particles, and volatile organic compounds that can impact respiratory health. However, the health risks differ qualitatively and quantitatively from traditional secondhand smoke and require ongoing research.

## Common Misconceptions about Secondhand Smoke
A widespread misconception is that secondhand smoke only harms people with prolonged exposure or that ventilation and opening windows can effectively eliminate risks. In reality, brief exposure still causes measurable damage and ventilation does not remove all toxic chemicals. Another myth is that smokeless tobacco and nicotine replacement therapies produce harmful secondhand smoke, which they typically do not. Clarifying these misconceptions supports informed risk reduction decisions.

## Limitations and Challenges in Studying Secondhand Smoke Effects
Studying the health impacts of SHS faces challenges including accurately measuring real-world exposure levels and controlling for confounding factors such as active smoking and other pollutants. Individual genetic susceptibility and co-morbid conditions also influence outcomes. Despite these limitations, consistent epidemiological findings and biological plausibility support causal links between SHS and numerous diseases.

## Practical Recommendations for Protecting Vulnerable Groups
Protecting children and pregnant women from secondhand smoke involves eliminating tobacco use inside homes and vehicles and creating smoke-free public spaces. Caregivers should advocate for smoke-free policies in schools and childcare settings. Healthcare providers can counsel families about risks and encourage smoking cessation. Public health messaging should emphasize that only complete avoidance, not partial measures, effectively reduces harm.

## Practical Guidance
- Avoid indoor environments where smoking is occurring to minimize exposure to secondhand smoke.
- Establish and enforce strict no-smoking rules inside homes and vehicles, especially when children or pregnant women are present.
- Support and comply with local and national smoke-free laws to protect community health.
- Encourage smokers to quit and seek support through counseling and cessation programs to reduce household smoke.
- Use air purifiers only as a supplementary measure; they cannot eliminate all harmful components of secondhand smoke.
- Educate family members, friends, and coworkers about the health risks associated with secondhand smoke exposure.
- Recognize symptoms such as coughing, eye irritation, or breathing difficulty that may be related to secondhand smoke and seek medical advice if persistent.
- Advocate for tobacco control measures and environmental protections in workplaces, public transport, and recreational areas.

## Safety Notes

- Children and pregnant women require special protection as they are particularly vulnerable to effects of secondhand smoke.

## Common Misconceptions
### Opening windows or using fans completely removes the harmful effects of secondhand smoke indoors.
**Reality:** Ventilation may reduce smoke concentration somewhat but does not eliminate all toxic chemicals or health risks associated with secondhand smoke exposure.

### Brief exposure to secondhand smoke is harmless to non-smokers.
**Reality:** Even short-term exposure can cause immediate physiological changes and contribute to disease risk over time.

### Electronic cigarettes produce the same harmful secondhand smoke as combustible tobacco products.
**Reality:** E-cigarettes produce aerosol, which contains fewer harmful chemicals than tobacco smoke, but exposure is not risk-free and is under ongoing study.

## Clinical Pearls

## Glossary
- **Secondhand Smoke (SHS):** Smoke involuntarily inhaled by non-smokers, including sidestream smoke from burning tobacco and mainstream smoke exhaled by smokers.
- **Environmental Tobacco Smoke (ETS):** Another term for secondhand smoke, referring to the mixture of smoke present in the air from tobacco products.
- **Sidestream Smoke:** Smoke emitted from the burning end of a cigarette or other tobacco product, usually more toxic than exhaled smoke.
- **Mainstream Smoke:** Smoke inhaled then exhaled by a smoker from a tobacco product.
- **Carcinogen:** Any substance capable of causing cancer in living tissue.
- **Nicotine:** An addictive chemical found in tobacco that affects the nervous system and cardiovascular function.
- **Asthma:** A chronic respiratory condition characterized by airway inflammation and reversible airflow obstruction.
- **Cotinine:** A metabolite of nicotine used as a biomarker to measure exposure to tobacco smoke.
