## Definition
Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) therapy is a medical treatment that delivers a constant stream of pressurized air through a mask to keep the upper airway open during sleep, primarily used to treat obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

## Why This Matters
Untreated sleep apnea leads to poor sleep quality, daytime fatigue, and increased risk of serious cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. CPAP therapy effectively reverses the airway obstruction, improving both symptoms and long-term health outcomes. Understanding CPAP empowers patients and caregivers to improve sleep health and adhere to treatment.

## Overview and Definition of CPAP Therapy
CPAP therapy involves the use of a device that delivers continuous positive air pressure via a mask worn over the nose or mouth. This positive pressure acts like a splint to prevent airway collapse during sleep in individuals affected by obstructive sleep apnea. The therapy is non-invasive and commonly prescribed by sleep specialists after diagnostic evaluation through polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing.

## Mechanism of Action and Physiology
During sleep, muscles of the upper airway relax, which can cause the airway to collapse in patients with obstructive sleep apnea. CPAP devices provide a steady pressure of air that maintains patency of the airway, preventing the repetitive episodes of airway closure and breathing pauses that characterize sleep apnea. This ongoing airway patency improves oxygenation and reduces strain on the heart and other organs.

## Indications and Patient Selection
CPAP therapy is indicated primarily for patients diagnosed with moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea based on sleep study findings. Indications include symptoms such as loud snoring, observed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness, and health complications like hypertension. Contraindications may include severe claustrophobia, some nasal obstructions, or certain rare respiratory disorders; evaluation by a healthcare provider is essential.

## Evaluation and Diagnostic Process
Candidates for CPAP therapy should undergo formal evaluation, including a clinical history and objective testing like an overnight polysomnography or home sleep apnea test. These tests measure the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen levels, and sleep architecture to diagnose and assess severity of sleep apnea, critical for appropriate therapy decisions.

## Practical Considerations: Device Components and Usage
A typical CPAP setup consists of a machine that generates air pressure, tubing that transports the air, and a mask that fits over the nose or nose and mouth. Mask types include nasal pillow, nasal mask, and full-face mask, selected based on patient preference and comfort. Users need education on proper fitting, adjustment, and troubleshooting common issues to facilitate adherence.

## Efficacy and Clinical Evidence
Robust clinical studies demonstrate CPAP therapy significantly reduces apnea events, improves oxygen saturation, and alleviates daytime sleepiness. Large randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support its benefits on cardiovascular outcomes and quality of life in obstructive sleep apnea patients. Effectiveness depends on adherence and proper pressure settings.

## Limitations and Challenges
Despite its effectiveness, CPAP therapy faces challenges including patient non-adherence due to discomfort, nasal congestion, mask leaks, or claustrophobia. Some patients find the equipment cumbersome, leading to early discontinuation. Alternative treatments like oral appliances or surgery may be considered for those intolerant to CPAP.

## Safety and Side Effects
CPAP is generally safe but may cause side effects such as nasal dryness, skin irritation at mask contact points, eye irritation from air leaks, or discomfort from pressure settings. Rare complications include aerophagia (air swallowing) and very rarely barotrauma. Proper device titration, mask selection, and follow-up are crucial to minimizing risks.

## Misconceptions About CPAP Therapy
Common misconceptions include beliefs that CPAP cures sleep apnea permanently (it does not; it manages symptoms), or that it is painful or dangerous. Another myth is that snoring alone always requires CPAP; however, treatment is indicated only when clinical and diagnostic criteria for sleep apnea are met.

## Maintenance and Long-Term Management
Regular cleaning and maintenance of CPAP components is vital to prevent infection and ensure optimal device function. Routine follow-up with healthcare providers helps monitor therapy effectiveness, pressure adjustment, and patient adherence. Technological advances now include smart devices with data tracking to support personalized care.

## Practical Guidance
- Always obtain a proper diagnostic assessment before starting CPAP therapy to confirm obstructive sleep apnea.
- Select a mask type that fits comfortably with minimal air leaks to improve adherence.
- Clean CPAP equipment daily or per manufacturer recommendations to prevent infections.
- Use humidification features if experiencing nasal dryness or irritation.
- Report side effects to your healthcare provider promptly for adjustments.
- Follow up regularly with the sleep specialist or provider to evaluate treatment effectiveness and pressure settings.
- Do not stop CPAP therapy abruptly without consulting a provider, as untreated sleep apnea has serious health risks.
- Consider lifestyle factors such as weight loss, avoiding alcohol, and sleeping position, which may complement CPAP therapy.

## Safety Notes
- Patients with respiratory or cardiac conditions should use CPAP under medical supervision.
- Do not use CPAP devices near water to avoid electrical hazards.
- Ensure proper voltage and device maintenance to prevent malfunction.
- Avoid using CPAP if experiencing a respiratory infection until cleared by a healthcare professional.
- Report any signs of skin breakdown or allergic reaction from CPAP materials promptly.

## Glossary
- **Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP):** A therapy that uses continuous air pressure delivered via a mask to keep the upper airway open in patients with obstructive sleep apnea.
- **Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA):** A common sleep disorder characterized by repeated episodes of complete or partial airway blockage during sleep, causing breathing pauses.
- **Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI):** A measure of the severity of sleep apnea, counting the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep.
- **Polysomnography:** An overnight sleep study that records brain waves, oxygen levels, heart rate, and breathing patterns to diagnose sleep disorders.
- **Hypopnea:** A partial blockage of the airway during sleep leading to reduced airflow but not complete stopping.
- **Humidifier (CPAP):** An optional device component that adds moisture to the air delivered by CPAP to reduce dryness and irritation.
- **Apnea:** A complete pause in breathing lasting at least 10 seconds during sleep.
- **Mask leak:** Air escaping from the edges of the CPAP mask that can reduce therapy effectiveness and cause discomfort.
