## Definition
Medication review is a comprehensive, systematic evaluation of all medications an older adult is taking, aiming to ensure appropriate use, optimize outcomes, minimize adverse effects, and improve adherence. This process typically involves assessing prescription drugs, over-the-counter medications, supplements, and herbal products.

## Why This Matters
Older adults often take multiple medications due to chronic health conditions, increasing the risk of harmful drug interactions, side effects, and medication errors. Medication review acts as a critical preventive and safety measure to address these risks, improving overall health, functionality, and quality of life in aging populations.

## Understanding Medication Review in Older Adults
Medication review refers to the thorough evaluation of all medications a patient is currently using to ensure they are still appropriate, safe, and effective. In older adults, this process is especially important due to the high prevalence of polypharmacy—commonly defined as taking five or more medications simultaneously—which raises the risk of adverse drug events, hospitalization, and decreased quality of life. The review examines each medication’s indication, efficacy, dosage, duration, potential interactions, and adherence. This process aligns with goals of personalized medicine and geriatric pharmacology, aiming to tailor therapy to the unique physiological needs of elderly patients.

## Physiological Changes in Aging Affecting Medications
Aging causes well-documented changes in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. For example, decreased gastric acid and slowed gastrointestinal motility can alter drug absorption. Body composition shifts include increased fat and decreased lean body mass and total body water, which affect drug distribution volumes. Liver metabolism, particularly phase I reactions mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes, is generally reduced, leading to prolonged half-life of some drugs. Renal function often declines, impairing drug clearance, especially for renally eliminated medications. These physiological changes increase sensitivity to many medications, necessitating careful dose adjustments and monitoring during medication review.

## Risks and Consequences of Polypharmacy in Older Adults
Polypharmacy elevates risks of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), drug-drug and drug-disease interactions, adverse drug reactions (ADRs), and medication non-adherence. Increased pill burden can cause confusion, leading to omission or duplication errors. ADRs may manifest as falls, cognitive impairment, or organ toxicity. Hospitalizations and healthcare costs rise with polypharmacy. Medication reviews aim to identify unnecessary or harmful medications to reduce these risks and optimize therapeutic benefit.

## Components and Process of Medication Review
A standardized medication review typically includes: compiling a complete and accurate medication list (prescription, OTC, supplements), verifying indications and clinical need for each drug, assessing effectiveness and side effects, checking for interactions, evaluating adherence, and considering patient preferences and goals. The review may lead to recommendations such as dose adjustments, discontinuation (deprescribing), substitution, or additional monitoring. It involves collaboration with patients, caregivers, pharmacists, and healthcare providers to ensure comprehensive assessment and shared decision making.

## Who Conducts Medication Reviews?
Medication reviews can be performed by clinical pharmacists, physicians (especially geriatricians), nurse practitioners, or interdisciplinary teams trained in geriatric care. Pharmacists often play a critical role due to their expertise in pharmacology and medication management. Collaborative care models have demonstrated improved outcomes. In some healthcare systems, specialized medication review clinics or programs are available for older adults, particularly after hospital discharge or during transitions of care to minimize medication-related problems.

## Evidence Supporting Medication Review Benefits
Multiple studies and systematic reviews suggest that medication review interventions in older adults can reduce inappropriate prescribing, decrease adverse drug events, lower hospitalization rates, and improve medication adherence and patient satisfaction. However, the quality of evidence varies, and benefits may depend on the setting, intervention intensity, and follow-up. Emerging evidence encourages integrating medication reviews into routine geriatric care to enhance safety and effectiveness of pharmacotherapy.

## Limitations and Challenges in Medication Review
Despite its recognized value, medication review faces challenges including incomplete medication histories due to patient recall issues or undocumented supplements, variability in reviewer expertise, time constraints, and inconsistent documentation. Some medications may require specialist input to appropriately deprescribe. Additionally, patient resistance to changing long-standing medications and lack of standardized protocols can hinder effective reviews. Continuous training and system-level support are necessary to overcome these barriers.

## Practical Guidance for Conducting Medication Reviews
Effective medication reviews involve comprehensive data collection, patient-centered communication, and clear documentation. Utilizing tools such as the Beers Criteria or STOPP/START criteria helps identify PIMs. Engaging patients and caregivers in discussions about medication goals and side effects enhances adherence and acceptance of changes. Follow-up planning ensures monitoring of outcomes and adjustments as needed. Coordination among healthcare providers is vital to avoid fragmentation of care and duplication of medications.

## Deprescribing in Older Adults
Deprescribing is the planned process of tapering or stopping medications that may no longer be beneficial or may cause harm, with the goal to reduce polypharmacy and its associated risks. Medication reviews often identify candidates for deprescribing, particularly when medications lack clear indications or when risks outweigh benefits. Successful deprescribing requires careful assessment, patient education, and monitoring for withdrawal symptoms or disease recurrence. It represents an important strategy within medication review to improve safety in elderly patients.

## Common Misconceptions About Medication Review
Some believe medication reviews only focus on reducing the number of medications or that they are only necessary after adverse events occur. Others think that patients should independently manage medication safety without provider involvement. In reality, medication review is a proactive, multidisciplinary process aimed at optimizing therapy—not merely minimizing drugs—and is recommended routinely as part of comprehensive geriatric care. Patients and caregivers play a collaborative role in reporting concerns and participating in decision making.

## Safety Considerations and Watchpoints
Safety is paramount during medication reviews. Attention must be given to medications with narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., warfarin, digoxin), drugs affecting cognition or balance (e.g., benzodiazepines, anticholinergics), and medications requiring kidney or liver function monitoring. Abrupt discontinuation of certain drugs can cause adverse withdrawal effects; therefore, tapering is often essential. Close monitoring after changes ensures early detection of issues. Documentation of review findings and communication with all care team members enhances patient safety.

## Practical Guidance
- Prepare a complete and accurate list of all medications, including OTCs and supplements.
- Use validated criteria (e.g., Beers, STOPP/START) to identify potentially inappropriate medications.
- Engage patients and caregivers to understand medication experiences and adherence issues.
- Coordinate with pharmacists and healthcare providers to ensure comprehensive review and shared decisions.
- Plan follow-up to monitor effects of medication changes, including deprescribing.
- Educate patients about the purpose and benefits of medication review to improve acceptance.
- Document review findings clearly in the medical record.
- Address barriers such as limited health literacy and cognitive impairment when reviewing medications.

## Safety Notes
- Avoid abrupt cessation of medications that require tapering to prevent withdrawal syndromes.
- Monitor closely for adverse drug reactions, especially with high-risk medications.
- Consider renal and hepatic function when recommending dose adjustments.
- Be cautious of medications that increase risk of falls and cognitive impairment in older adults.
- Ensure communication among all healthcare providers to prevent duplication or conflicting prescriptions.

## Glossary
- **Medication Review:** A structured and systematic evaluation of all medications a patient is currently using to optimize therapy and minimize harm.
- **Polypharmacy:** The simultaneous use of multiple medications by a patient, often defined as five or more drugs, commonly seen in older adults.
- **Deprescribing:** The planned process of withdrawing or reducing medications that are unnecessary, inappropriate, or causing harm.
- **Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR):** An unwanted or harmful reaction experienced after the administration of a medication.
- **Pharmacokinetics:** The study of how a drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated by the body.
- **Pharmacodynamics:** The study of the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their mechanisms of action.
- **Beers Criteria:** A guideline developed to identify potentially inappropriate medications for use in older adults.
- **STOPP/START Criteria:** Evidence-based screening tools to detect potentially inappropriate prescriptions (STOPP) and potential prescribing omissions (START) in older patients.
- **Complementary and Alternative Medicines (CAM):** Medicinal products and practices that are not considered part of conventional Western medicine such as herbal supplements and acupuncture.
