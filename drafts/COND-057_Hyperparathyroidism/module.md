## Definition
Hyperparathyroidism is a medical condition where one or more of the parathyroid glands produce too much parathyroid hormone (PTH), leading to elevated calcium levels in the blood (hypercalcemia) and altered bone metabolism.

## Why This Matters
Because PTH regulates critical minerals that affect bone strength, kidney function, and nerve/muscle activity, hyperparathyroidism can result in serious health problems including osteoporosis, kidney stones, cognitive issues, and cardiovascular disease if untreated. Early recognition and appropriate management improve outcomes and quality of life.

## Definition and Types of Hyperparathyroidism
Hyperparathyroidism is defined by excess synthesis and secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH) by the parathyroid glands. There are three major types: primary, secondary, and tertiary. Primary hyperparathyroidism occurs when one or more parathyroid glands develop a benign tumor (adenoma), hyperplasia, or rarely carcinoma, causing autonomous overproduction of PTH. Secondary hyperparathyroidism results from chronic hypocalcemia often due to chronic kidney disease or vitamin D deficiency, leading to compensatory overproduction of PTH to maintain calcium balance. Tertiary hyperparathyroidism arises when the parathyroid glands become autonomously overactive after prolonged secondary stimulation, commonly in advanced kidney disease. Each type has distinct underlying mechanisms and clinical implications.

## Physiology and Mechanisms of Parathyroid Hormone
Parathyroid hormone (PTH) secreted by parathyroid glands regulates serum calcium and phosphorus levels. PTH increases calcium release from bones by activating osteoclasts, enhances calcium reabsorption in kidneys, and promotes activation of vitamin D in kidneys, which increases intestinal calcium absorption. It also reduces phosphate reabsorption in the kidneys resulting in increased phosphate excretion. This tightly regulated system maintains serum calcium within a narrow range essential for muscular, skeletal, and neurological functions. Dysregulation as in hyperparathyroidism leads to sustained hypercalcemia and metabolic derangements.

## Causes and Risk Factors
Primary hyperparathyroidism is most commonly caused by a single benign parathyroid adenoma (approximately 80-85% of cases), less frequently by parathyroid hyperplasia or carcinoma. Risk factors include female gender, age over 50, radiation exposure to neck, and genetic syndromes such as Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia types 1 and 2 (MEN1, MEN2). Secondary hyperparathyroidism is primarily caused by chronic kidney disease or vitamin D deficiency resulting in hypocalcemia-driven PTH secretion. Tertiary hyperparathyroidism arises after prolonged secondary hyperparathyroidism, especially in those on long term dialysis.

## Symptoms and Clinical Presentation
Many patients with hyperparathyroidism are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally on routine blood tests. Symptomatic patients may experience kidney stones (nephrolithiasis), bone pain and fractures (due to osteoporosis and osteitis fibrosa cystica), abdominal pain, nausea, fatigue, muscle weakness, psychiatric symptoms such as depression and cognitive impairment, and cardiac manifestations (arrhythmias, hypertension). Symptoms can be subtle or overlap with other disorders, making clinical suspicion important in at-risk groups.

## Diagnostic Evaluation
Evaluation includes blood tests for serum calcium (usually elevated in primary hyperparathyroidism), intact PTH levels (inappropriately high in primary), serum phosphate, vitamin D levels, renal function tests, and urinary calcium excretion. Imaging modalities such as neck ultrasound and sestamibi nuclear scans help localize abnormal parathyroid glands pre-surgically. Bone mineral density testing is also important to assess skeletal involvement. Secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism diagnosis is based on biochemical and clinical context, with elevated PTH but low or normal calcium.

## Management and Treatment
Treatment depends on type, severity, symptoms, and complications. Primary hyperparathyroidism with symptomatic or significant hypercalcemia often requires parathyroidectomy (surgical removal of abnormal glands). Asymptomatic patients meeting criteria regarding calcium levels, bone density, and kidney function may also be surgical candidates. Medical management options include bisphosphonates to protect bones, calcimimetics (e.g., cinacalcet) to reduce PTH secretion, and vitamin D supplementation when deficient. Secondary hyperparathyroidism treatment focuses on correcting underlying causes like kidney disease and vitamin D deficiency, dietary phosphate restriction, and medications to control PTH. Tertiary hyperparathyroidism often requires surgery if autonomous gland hyperfunction persists.

## Lifestyle and Dietary Considerations
Patients with hyperparathyroidism benefit from staying well hydrated to reduce kidney stone risk, maintaining a balanced intake of calcium and vitamin D as recommended by healthcare providers, and avoiding excessive calcium supplementation. Monitoring bone health through weight-bearing exercise and avoiding smoking/alcohol abuse improve bone strength. Dietary phosphate management is important especially in secondary hyperparathyroidism related to kidney disease. Regular medical follow-up is necessary to adjust these recommendations.

## Complications and Prognosis
If untreated, hyperparathyroidism can lead to serious complications including osteoporosis with fractures, nephrolithiasis, kidney failure, cardiovascular disease due to hypertension and arrhythmias, pancreatitis, neurocognitive decline, and increased mortality. Prognosis after appropriate treatment, particularly surgical removal of abnormal parathyroid tissue, is generally excellent with resolution or improvement of symptoms and normalization of calcium levels. Early detection and management improve long-term outcomes significantly.

## Misconceptions about Hyperparathyroidism
A common misconception is that all patients with hyperparathyroidism have obvious symptoms; in reality many are asymptomatic. Another misunderstanding is that surgery is always necessary, whereas medical management may be preferable or adequate in some cases. Some believe high calcium intake causes hyperparathyroidism, but the condition is due to gland overactivity, not dietary calcium excess. Also, high PTH is sometimes confused with low calcium; in hyperparathyroidism, calcium is usually elevated. Understanding these helps patients and healthcare providers avoid delays or errors in treatment.

## Safety Notes and Monitoring
Patients should avoid self-medicating with calcium or vitamin D supplements without medical guidance as excessive supplementation can worsen hypercalcemia. Regular monitoring of serum calcium, PTH, kidney function, and bone density is essential to detect progression or complications. Surgical treatment risks include hypocalcemia post-operatively requiring calcium/vitamin D therapy. Patients on medications like calcimimetics must be monitored for side effects such as hypocalcemia. Collaboration with endocrinologists or nephrologists is often necessary.

## Practical Guidance
- If you have elevated calcium or PTH levels on blood tests, seek specialist evaluation for hyperparathyroidism.
- Understand that some cases may not cause symptoms but require monitoring to prevent complications.
- Follow hydration and dietary recommendations provided by healthcare providers to manage symptoms and reduce kidney stone risk.
- Discuss treatment options including surgery and medications with your healthcare provider based on your diagnosis type and severity.
- Avoid self-supplementation with calcium or vitamin D unless recommended by your doctor.
- Regularly monitor bone health and kidney function through tests as advised.
- Report new symptoms such as muscle weakness, bone pain, or cognitive changes promptly.
- Inform your healthcare team of all medications you are using to avoid drug interactions and side effects.

## Safety Notes
- Avoid excessive calcium and vitamin D supplements without medical supervision as they can worsen hypercalcemia.
- Surgical treatment can cause hypocalcemia requiring close monitoring and supplementation.
- Calcimimetic medications may cause side effects including low calcium levels; blood tests are essential during treatment.
- Patients with kidney disease need individualized management to control secondary hyperparathyroidism safely.
- Notify healthcare professionals about any new symptoms or changes during treatment to ensure safety.

## Common Misconceptions
### All patients with hyperparathyroidism show clear symptoms like kidney stones or bone pain.
**Reality:** Many patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally on blood tests.

### Surgery is the only treatment for hyperparathyroidism.
**Reality:** Some cases can be managed medically depending on the type and severity.

### High dietary calcium causes hyperparathyroidism.
**Reality:** Hyperparathyroidism is caused by overproduction of PTH, not excessive calcium intake.

### High PTH always means low calcium levels.
**Reality:** In primary hyperparathyroidism, PTH is high alongside elevated calcium.

## Clinical Pearls
- Consider hyperparathyroidism in patients with unexplained hypercalcemia or kidney stones.
- Differentiate types of hyperparathyroidism using biochemical and clinical context for proper management.
- Preoperative localization of adenomas improves surgical outcomes.
- Maintain adequate hydration to reduce kidney stone risk in hyperparathyroid patients.
- Regularly assess bone mineral density since osteoporosis is a common complication.
- Medical therapy with calcimimetics is an option for patients who are poor surgical candidates.
- Vitamin D deficiency should be corrected cautiously to avoid worsening hypercalcemia.
- Monitor serum calcium closely during treatment to prevent complications.

## Glossary
- **Hyperparathyroidism:** A condition with excess secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH), causing elevated blood calcium levels.
- **Parathyroid hormone (PTH):** A hormone controlling calcium and phosphorus metabolism, produced by parathyroid glands.
- **Parathyroid adenoma:** A benign tumor of a parathyroid gland leading to excess PTH production.
- **Hypercalcemia:** Higher than normal levels of calcium in the blood.
- **Osteoporosis:** A disorder characterized by weakened bones and increased fracture risk.
- **Nephrolithiasis:** The formation of kidney stones.
- **Calcimimetics:** Medications that activate calcium-sensing receptors to reduce PTH secretion.
