## Definition
Medicines in older adults encompass all pharmaceutical therapies and their use considerations in individuals typically aged 65 years and older, accounting for age-related alterations in drug handling, effectiveness, and safety.

## Why This Matters
As populations age globally, the use of medicines in older adults has become increasingly critical due to physiological changes that alter drug effects, increased susceptibility to side effects, and frequent use of multiple medications (polypharmacy). Inappropriate medication use can lead to adverse outcomes, hospitalizations, and decreased quality of life, making education on proper medication management essential.

## Overview of Medicines Use in Older Adults
Medicines in older adults are prescribed to manage chronic conditions common with aging such as hypertension, diabetes, arthritis, and heart disease. The aging process affects both how drugs are processed by the body (pharmacokinetics) and how drugs affect the body (pharmacodynamics). This necessitates tailored medication choices, doses, and close monitoring to maintain efficacy and avoid harm.

## Pharmacokinetics Changes in Older Adults
Pharmacokinetics (PK)—the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of drugs—is often altered in older adults. Absorption usually remains unchanged, but distribution is affected due to increased body fat and decreased total body water and lean muscle mass. Metabolism may slow because of reduced hepatic blood flow and enzyme activity. Elimination, particularly renal clearance, often declines due to decreased kidney function (glomerular filtration rate). These changes can increase drug half-life and plasma concentrations, requiring dose adjustments.

## Pharmacodynamics Changes in Older Adults
Pharmacodynamics (PD) refers to drug effects and the body's response to medications. Aging influences receptor sensitivity, signal transduction, and homeostatic mechanisms. For example, beta-adrenergic receptor responsiveness decreases, altering cardiovascular responses, while increased sensitivity to central nervous system depressants can enhance sedation and cognitive effects. Understanding these changes helps in predicting altered drug responses and side effect risks in older adults.

## Polypharmacy and Its Risks
Polypharmacy is commonly defined as the use of five or more medications concurrently. It is prevalent in older adults due to multiple chronic conditions. Polypharmacy increases the risk of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), drug–drug interactions, medication non-adherence, and hospitalizations. It also complicates clinical management and can lead to prescribing cascades, where new drugs are prescribed to treat side effects of existing medications.

## Common Medication Classes and Considerations in Older Adults
Certain drug classes require special attention in older adults due to heightened risks. For example, benzodiazepines and anticholinergics can cause sedation, confusion, and falls. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) increase gastrointestinal bleeding and kidney injury risk. Cardiovascular drugs such as digoxin need careful dose adjustments. The Beers Criteria and STOPP/START criteria guide clinicians on potentially inappropriate medications in this population.

## Monitoring and Safety Practices
Monitoring therapy in older adults involves regular review of medications for efficacy, side effects, and interactions. Laboratory tests (e.g., kidney and liver function), vital sign checks, and patient-reported symptoms are essential. Safety practices include deprescribing unnecessary medicines, using simplest effective regimens, educating patients on side effect recognition, and promoting adherence strategies such as pill organizers.

## Impact of Kidney and Liver Function on Drug Use
Kidney and liver functions often decline with age, affecting drug clearance and metabolism, respectively. Reduced kidney function slows elimination of renally-excreted drugs like digoxin, lithium, and some antibiotics, increasing toxicity risk. Liver impairment alters metabolism, notably phase I reactions (oxidation, reduction) more than phase II (conjugation), affecting drugs like diazepam. Estimating renal function (e.g., using creatinine clearance or eGFR) is critical for dosing.

## Deprescribing and Medication Review Strategies
Deprescribing is the planned process of tapering or stopping medicines that may no longer provide benefit or cause harm. It involves evaluating all medications for necessity, effectiveness, and safety. Medication reviews with healthcare providers, pharmacists, or interdisciplinary teams are vital to identify candidates for deprescribing and reduce polypharmacy risks, improving outcomes and reducing adverse events.

## Misconceptions About Medicines in Older Adults
Common misconceptions include the belief that older adults cannot take certain medications at all, that side effects are inevitable and unpreventable, or that all drugs should be stopped with aging. Another false belief is that prescribing more medicines always means better care. Clarifying these helps improve communication and encourages proper medicine use and monitoring.

## Role of Caregivers and Healthcare Teams
Caregivers often support medication management through reminders, organizing dosing, monitoring for side effects, and communicating with healthcare providers. Interdisciplinary healthcare teams including physicians, pharmacists, nurses, and social workers collaborate to optimize medication regimens tailored for older adults’ needs, enhancing safety and adherence.

## Tools and Resources for Medication Management
Several tools support medication safety in older adults, including the Beers Criteria, STOPP/START criteria, medication reconciliation protocols, and patient education materials focused on adherence and recognizing adverse effects. Digital apps and pill organizers assist with regimen complexity. These resources facilitate safer medication use and empower older adults and caregivers.

## Balancing Benefits and Risks: Individualized Medicine Use
Because older adults have varying health statuses, comorbidities, and goals of care, medication decisions must be personalized. Benefits of symptom control, disease prevention, and quality of life must be balanced against risks of side effects and interactions. Shared decision-making between patients, families, and healthcare providers promotes optimal outcomes.

## Practical Guidance
- Discuss all current medications with healthcare providers regularly to identify potential risks or unnecessary drugs.
- Inform providers about any side effects or new symptoms promptly for medication reassessment.
- Use one pharmacy when possible to help track all medications and potential interactions.
- Employ tools like pill organizers, medication lists, or reminders to improve adherence.
- Ask about kidney and liver function tests and how they relate to medication dosing.
- Review the need for each medication periodically with the healthcare team to consider deprescribing.
- Avoid over-the-counter medications and supplements without consulting a healthcare professional due to interaction risks.
- Report any episodes of dizziness, confusion, falls, or unusual symptoms immediately as they may signal medication-related problems.

## Safety Notes
- Never adjust medication doses without consulting a healthcare provider.
- Be aware that over-the-counter drugs and herbal supplements can interact with prescription medicines.
- Use caution with sedatives, tranquilizers, and opioids as they increase fall and cognitive impairment risks in older adults.
- Ensure kidney and liver function are regularly evaluated for dose adjustments.
- Avoid suddenly stopping medications without provider guidance to prevent withdrawal or disease exacerbation.
- Be vigilant for signs of adverse drug reactions including confusion, weakness, gastrointestinal issues, or allergic reactions.

## Common Misconceptions
### All medicines are unsafe for older adults.
**Reality:** Many medicines are safe and beneficial when used appropriately and monitored carefully in older adults.

### Older adults should stop taking medications as they age to avoid side effects.
**Reality:** Medications should be reviewed and adjusted as needed but not stopped arbitrarily, as this can worsen health conditions.

### Polypharmacy always means inappropriate prescribing.
**Reality:** While polypharmacy increases risks, multiple medicines are sometimes necessary for managing complex conditions.

### Side effects in older adults cannot be prevented.
**Reality:** Many side effects can be minimized through careful drug selection, dose adjustment, and monitoring.

## Clinical Pearls
- Regular medication review is critical for preventing adverse events in older adults.
- Estimate renal function routinely to adjust doses of renally-cleared drugs.
- Use established criteria (Beers, STOPP/START) to identify potentially inappropriate medications.
- Educate patients and caregivers to recognize early warning signs of drug toxicity.
- Deprescribing can improve quality of life and reduce hospitalization risks.

## Glossary
- **Pharmacokinetics (PK):** The study of how a drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated by the body.
- **Pharmacodynamics (PD):** How the drug affects the body, including mechanisms of action and the relationship between drug concentration and effect.
- **Polypharmacy:** The concurrent use of multiple medications by a patient, often defined as five or more.
- **Deprescribing:** The planned process of reducing or stopping medications that may not be beneficial or might be harmful.
- **Beers Criteria:** A list of potentially inappropriate medications to be avoided or used cautiously in older adults.
- **STOPP/START Criteria:** Screening tools used to identify potentially inappropriate prescriptions (STOPP) and potential prescribing omissions (START) in older adults.
- **Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR):** An unwanted or harmful reaction experienced following the administration of a drug or combination of drugs.
- **Creatinine Clearance (CrCl):** A measure of kidney function used to estimate how well the kidneys are removing creatinine from the blood.
