## Definition
Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) is menstrual blood loss that is excessive in volume or duration and negatively affects a woman's quality of life. It is often clinically defined as blood loss exceeding 80 milliliters per menstrual cycle or bleeding lasting longer than seven days.

## Why This Matters
Heavy menstrual bleeding can substantially impact physical health—through anemia and fatigue—and emotional and social well-being. Understanding, diagnosing, and managing HMB is important to improve quality of life, prevent complications such as iron deficiency, and address underlying health conditions effectively.

## Definition and Clinical Description of Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB), also known as menorrhagia, refers to excessive or prolonged menstrual bleeding that interferes with a woman’s physical, emotional, social, or material quality of life. It typically involves menstrual blood loss of more than 80 milliliters per cycle or bleeding lasting longer than 7 days. Common terms used synonymously include excessive menstrual bleeding and abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB), although AUB is a broader category that includes other menstrual irregularities.

## Physiology of Normal Menstrual Bleeding
Normal menstrual bleeding results from the cyclical shedding of the endometrium (uterine lining) regulated by the hormonal interplay of estrogen and progesterone. The process includes vasoconstriction of endometrial blood vessels, activation of coagulation pathways, and controlled tissue breakdown. A typical menstrual period lasts 3 to 7 days with an average blood loss of 30-40 milliliters.

## Mechanisms and Causes of Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
Heavy menstrual bleeding arises when there is disruption in the normal balance of endometrial repair, hormonal regulation, or coagulation. Major causes include structural abnormalities such as uterine fibroids (leiomyomas), polyps, adenomyosis; hormonal disorders including anovulation; bleeding disorders like von Willebrand disease; intrauterine devices; infections; and rarely, malignancies such as endometrial carcinoma. Idiopathic cases, where no clear cause is found, are also common.

## Risk Factors for Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
Risk factors for HMB include age (more common during adolescence and perimenopause), obesity, family history of bleeding disorders, use of hormonal contraception or anticoagulant medications, uterine abnormalities, and certain systemic diseases like liver or kidney disease. Awareness of these factors aids in risk stratification and targeted evaluation.

## Symptoms and Impact on Quality of Life
Symptoms of heavy menstrual bleeding include visibly heavy or prolonged bleeding, passage of large blood clots, frequent changing of sanitary products, and symptoms of anemia such as fatigue, weakness, or shortness of breath. HMB can lead to absenteeism from work or school, reduced social interactions, and psychological distress.

## Evaluation and Diagnostic Work-up
Evaluation involves detailed menstrual history including duration, flow, and impact; physical examination focusing on pelvic and abdominal findings; laboratory tests such as complete blood count and coagulation studies; and imaging studies like pelvic ultrasound to identify structural causes. Endometrial sampling may be indicated in women over 35 or with risk factors for malignancy to exclude cancer.

## Medical Management of Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
Medical treatments include hormonal therapies such as combined oral contraceptives and progestins to regulate cycles and reduce bleeding; tranexamic acid, an antifibrinolytic that reduces bleeding volume; nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) which decrease prostaglandin-mediated bleeding; and treatment of underlying disorders such as bleeding abnormalities. Medical therapy is usually first-line, especially for women desiring fertility preservation.

## Surgical Treatment Options
When medical management is ineffective or contraindicated, surgical treatments include endometrial ablation—destruction of the uterine lining to reduce bleeding—and hysterectomy, the removal of the uterus, which provides definitive treatment. Other surgeries target structural causes, such as myomectomy for fibroids. Surgical options depend on patient preference, reproductive plans, and overall health.

## Lifestyle and Supportive Measures
Lifestyle interventions involve adequate nutrition rich in iron to prevent or treat anemia, stress management, and maintaining a healthy weight. Supportive care includes counseling and education about menstrual hygiene management and monitoring symptoms to identify worsening conditions needing medical attention.

## Safety Considerations and When to Seek Medical Care
Patients with heavy menstrual bleeding should seek medical care if they experience symptoms of anemia (extreme fatigue, dizziness), pass large clots frequently, or if bleeding worsens abruptly. Safety considerations include ruling out pregnancy-related bleeding, infections, or malignancy, and careful evaluation before starting hormonal or surgical treatments.

## Common Misconceptions about Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
A common misconception is that heavy menstrual bleeding is normal and cannot be treated. Another is that only older women experience HMB. Some believe natural remedies alone can cure HMB, which is not supported by strong evidence. Educating patients about the clinical significance and management options improves outcomes.

## Emerging and Uncertain Evidence in Heavy Menstrual Bleeding
Emerging evidence explores new hormonal agents, minimally invasive surgical techniques, and better diagnostic biomarkers to personalize treatment. However, the heterogeneity of causes challenges uniform treatment. Research continues on optimal management strategies balancing efficacy, safety, and fertility preservation.

## Practical Guidance
- Track menstrual bleeding patterns using diaries or apps to accurately report symptoms.
- Seek medical evaluation if bleeding is heavier than usual, associated with anemia symptoms, or if menses last longer than 7 days.
- Discuss all medications and supplements with your healthcare provider as some can affect bleeding.
- Iron-rich diet or supplementation may be necessary to prevent or treat anemia associated with heavy bleeding.
- Use prescribed medical therapies as directed and communicate any side effects to your clinician.
- Consider individual factors such as desire for pregnancy when discussing treatment options.
- Maintain good menstrual hygiene to prevent secondary infections.
- Follow up regularly to monitor response to treatment and adjust management as needed.

## Safety Notes
- Do not self-diagnose or self-treat heavy menstrual bleeding without consulting a healthcare provider.
- Avoid stopping prescribed hormonal therapies abruptly without medical advice.
- Be aware that some causes of heavy bleeding require urgent intervention (e.g., pregnancy complications).
- Inform your clinician about any pre-existing bleeding or clotting disorders.
- Medical management should consider contraindications such as thromboembolic risk before prescribing hormones.

## Common Misconceptions
### Heavy menstrual bleeding is just a normal part of being a woman and does not need medical attention.
**Reality:** Heavy menstrual bleeding can lead to serious health issues like anemia and impact quality of life, and effective treatments are available.

### Only older women or women nearing menopause experience heavy menstrual bleeding.
**Reality:** HMB can occur at any age but is also common in adolescents and women of reproductive age due to various causes.

### Natural remedies alone can cure heavy menstrual bleeding.
**Reality:** While some lifestyle measures support management, medical evaluation and treatment are often necessary to address underlying causes effectively.

## Clinical Pearls
- A detailed menstrual history and bleeding assessment tool can improve diagnosis accuracy.
- Tranexamic acid is especially useful in women who prefer non-hormonal options.
- Anemia is a common but often under-recognized complication of heavy menstrual bleeding.
- Pelvic ultrasound is a key non-invasive tool to identify structural causes of HMB.
- Tailor treatment plans to patient age, reproductive desires, and preferences for best outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- Heavy menstrual bleeding is defined by excessive menstrual blood loss impacting quality of life.
- Causes of HMB include structural uterine abnormalities, hormonal imbalances, bleeding disorders, infections, and malignancies.
- Accurate diagnosis requires systematic history-taking, physical exam, lab tests, and imaging.
- Medical treatments include hormonal therapy, tranexamic acid, and NSAIDs; surgery is reserved for refractory cases.
- Lifestyle factors like nutrition and stress can affect symptoms but do not replace medical treatment.
- HMB can cause iron-deficiency anemia requiring monitoring and intervention.
- Early evaluation of heavy menstrual bleeding improves quality of life and prevents complications.
- Misconceptions about HMB can delay diagnosis and treatment; education is essential.

## Glossary
- **Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (HMB):** Excessive menstrual blood loss that interferes with a woman's quality of life, typically over 80 milliliters per cycle or bleeding lasting more than 7 days.
- **Menorrhagia:** Medical term for heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Endometrium:** The lining of the uterus that thickens and sheds during the menstrual cycle.
- **Anemia:** A condition characterized by low levels of red blood cells or hemoglobin, often due to blood loss.
- **Leiomyomas (Uterine Fibroids):** Non-cancerous growths in the uterus that can cause heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Tranexamic Acid:** A medication that helps reduce bleeding by preventing the breakdown of blood clots.
- **Endometrial Ablation:** A surgical procedure that destroys the uterine lining to reduce menstrual bleeding.
- **NSAIDs:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, used to reduce pain and bleeding during menstruation.
