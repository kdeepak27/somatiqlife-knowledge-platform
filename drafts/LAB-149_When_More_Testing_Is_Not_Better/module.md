## Definition
'When More Testing Is Not Better' refers to the understanding that increasing the number or frequency of laboratory or consumer health tests beyond appropriate clinical indications does not necessarily improve diagnostic accuracy or patient outcomes and can instead lead to unintended negative consequences such as false positives, overdiagnosis, psychological harm, and unnecessary treatments.

## Why This Matters
Laboratory and consumer testing are powerful tools to detect, diagnose, and monitor health conditions. However, indiscriminate use of tests can create more problems than benefits. Excessive testing may yield confusing or misleading results, increase healthcare costs, and expose individuals to unnecessary risks, including invasive procedures prompted by false alarms. Patients empowered to understand when testing is beneficial versus excessive can advocate for appropriate care and avoid the pitfalls associated with overtesting. For healthcare providers, balancing test availability with evidence-based need is essential to optimize quality of care while minimizing harm and waste.

## Practical Guidance
- Discuss test necessity and purpose with your healthcare provider before proceeding.
- Avoid repetitive testing unless clinically indicated by symptoms or treatment changes.
- Understand that normal variability in lab results can occur; one abnormal test often requires confirmation.
- Beware that more tests increase chance of false positive results and unnecessary worry.
- Use reputable consumer health tests with proven reliability where applicable.
- Focus testing on evidence-based guidelines rather than blanket screening or curiosity-driven checks.
- Keep an updated list of your previous test results to prevent unnecessary repetition.
- Be cautious about insurance-driven or incentive-driven testing unless clinically justified.

## Safety Notes
- Most laboratory and consumer tests carry minimal direct risks but may lead to indirect harms through follow-up procedures.
- False positives can cause anxiety, unnecessary treatments, or exposure to invasive diagnostics.
- Understand radiation risks if abnormal tests lead to imaging studies.
- Economic costs and access disparities should be considered in test utilization.
- Consult healthcare professionals for test interpretation to avoid misunderstanding results.

## Common Misconceptions
### More testing always leads to better detection and outcomes.
**Reality:** Excessive testing can increase false positives and overdiagnosis without improving health outcomes.

### A single abnormal test result always means there is disease.
**Reality:** Biological variability and measurement errors mean that isolated abnormal results often do not indicate illness.

### Consumer health tests are always accurate and replace clinician testing.
**Reality:** Many consumer tests have variable accuracy and should not replace professional medical evaluation.

## Glossary
- **False Positive:** A test result indicating a condition is present when it is actually absent.
- **False Negative:** A test result indicating no condition is present when it actually is.
- **Sensitivity:** The ability of a test to correctly identify those with the disease (true positive rate).
- **Specificity:** The ability of a test to correctly identify those without the disease (true negative rate).
- **Overdiagnosis:** Diagnosis of a disease that will not cause symptoms or harm during a person's lifetime.
- **Screening Test:** A test performed on asymptomatic people to detect disease early.
- **Diagnostic Test:** A test used to confirm or rule out disease in symptomatic individuals.
- **Consumer Health Tests:** Health-related tests that can be performed by individuals outside traditional healthcare settings, such as home kits.
