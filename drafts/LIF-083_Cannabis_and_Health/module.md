## Definition
Cannabis refers to a genus of flowering plants whose dried flowers and extracts are used both recreationally and medicinally. The plant contains over 100 cannabinoids, with tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) as the principal psychoactive ingredient and cannabidiol (CBD) noted for its therapeutic potential without intoxication. Cannabis is consumed in various forms including smoking, vaporizing, edibles, and oils. It interacts primarily with the endocannabinoid system, influencing numerous physiological and neurological processes.

## Why This Matters
As cannabis use becomes more widespread due to changing legal status and social acceptance, understanding its health impacts is essential for making informed decisions. This knowledge enables consumers, healthcare providers, and policymakers to balance medicinal benefits against potential harms, apply effective harm reduction strategies, and dispel common misconceptions. Recognizing individual variability in response and risks fosters safer use and better health outcomes.

## Definitions and Terminology of Cannabis
Cannabis refers to the plant genus Cannabis, primarily Cannabis sativa, Cannabis indica, and hybrids thereof. Common terms include marijuana, weed, pot, ganja, and hashish (the concentrated resin). Key chemical constituents are cannabinoids, with THC (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol) causing psychoactive effects and CBD (cannabidiol) producing non-intoxicating therapeutic effects. Other minor cannabinoids include cannabigerol (CBG) and cannabinol (CBN). Products vary in THC and CBD concentration, affecting potency and effects. Understanding terminology helps clarify research findings, regulations, and health communications.

## The Endocannabinoid System and Mechanisms of Action
The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is a biological network comprised of cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2), endogenous cannabinoids (endocannabinoids), and enzymes regulating their synthesis and degradation. CB1 receptors are largely located in the brain and central nervous system, mediating psychoactive effects, while CB2 receptors predominate in immune cells, influencing inflammation. Cannabis cannabinoids mimic or modulate endocannabinoids to affect mood, pain, appetite, immune response, and neuroprotection. Understanding ECS function elucidates how cannabis produces both therapeutic and adverse effects.

## Pharmacokinetics and Routes of Cannabis Administration
Cannabis can be consumed via inhalation (smoking, vaporizing), oral ingestion (edibles, capsules), sublingual, topical, or transdermal routes. Absorption rate, onset, duration, and bioavailability differ by method; for example, inhalation leads to rapid onset (minutes) but shorter duration, while oral ingestion has delayed onset (1-2 hours) with prolonged effects. Metabolism primarily occurs in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes, producing active and inactive metabolites. Routes influence risk profiles, with smoked cannabis linked to respiratory irritation, while edibles carry risk of overconsumption due to delayed effects.

## Potential Therapeutic Uses of Cannabis
Emerging evidence supports the use of cannabis or cannabinoids for conditions like chronic pain, multiple sclerosis spasticity, chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, and certain forms of epilepsy (e.g., Dravet syndrome). CBD has demonstrated anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory properties in preliminary studies. Authorized medical cannabis products undergo regulatory approval with standardized dosages, often via oral or vaporized administration. However, evidence strength varies by condition, with many uses classified as moderate or emerging due to methodological limitations and inconsistent results.

## Health Risks and Adverse Effects of Cannabis Use
Cannabis use carries known risks including cognitive impairment, altered judgment, anxiety or paranoia, increased risk of psychosis especially in genetically predisposed individuals, and respiratory issues from smoking. Heavy or early-onset use is associated with increased risk of cannabis use disorder and negative impacts on academic and occupational outcomes. Use during adolescence is particularly concerning due to brain development. Adverse cardiovascular effects such as tachycardia and fluctuations in blood pressure have also been reported. Risks generally increase with higher THC potency and frequent use.

## Cannabis Use Disorder and Dependency
Cannabis use disorder (CUD) is characterized by problematic cannabis use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, including tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and unsuccessful efforts to cut down. Estimated prevalence varies but may affect approximately 9% of users overall, with higher rates among daily users and those who start in adolescence. Treatment approaches include behavioral therapies; pharmacological interventions are under investigation but no medications are currently FDA-approved specifically for CUD. Early identification and intervention can improve outcomes.

## Impact of Cannabis on Mental Health
Cannabis effects on mental health are complex. While some users report relief of anxiety or depression symptoms, evidence is mixed, with some studies indicating increased risk of anxiety, depression, and especially psychotic disorders in vulnerable individuals. High-THC cannabis can provoke transient psychotic symptoms or exacerbate existing schizophrenia. The relationship depends on dose, frequency, age of initiation, and genetic predisposition. Cautious use is advised for individuals with personal or family history of mental illness.

## Cannabis Use During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
Current evidence suggests cannabis use during pregnancy poses risks including low birth weight, preterm birth, and potential neurodevelopmental effects on the child. THC crosses the placental barrier and is also present in breast milk. Due to limited and mixed data, leading health organizations advise avoiding cannabis use during pregnancy and breastfeeding. More research is needed to clarify long-term developmental consequences.

## Risk Reduction and Harm Minimization Strategies
Risk reduction approaches encourage users to delay initiation, especially in adolescents; choose low-THC, high-CBD products; avoid synthetic cannabinoids; and opt for non-smoking routes like vaporizers or edibles while being cautious of delayed effects and dosing errors. Users should avoid driving or operating machinery after use. Mental health history should be considered before cannabis use. Education about product potency, sourcing from regulated providers, and awareness of legal status contribute to safer consumption.

## Common Misconceptions About Cannabis Safety and Effects
Popular misconceptions include beliefs that cannabis is completely harmless, non-addictive, or universally beneficial for mental health. While cannabis has therapeutic potential, it is not risk-free and can cause dependency and mental health issues in some users. Some assume natural origin means safety; however, contaminants and synthetic analogs pose health threats. Another misconception is that CBD is intoxicating or that all cannabis products produce similar effects; these are inaccurate—CBD is non-intoxicating and products vary widely in chemical composition and potency.

## Legal Status and Implications for Public Health
Cannabis legality varies worldwide—from prohibition to medical-only access to full recreational legalization. Legal status influences patterns of use, research feasibility, and regulation of product quality and potency. Jurisdictions with regulated markets may reduce illicit trade and improve harm reduction messaging. However, increased availability can lead to escalated use rates and potential public health challenges including impaired driving and adolescent access. Ongoing surveillance and policy evaluation are vital to balance individual freedom with community health safety.

## Practical Guidance
- If considering cannabis use for medical purposes, consult a qualified healthcare provider for personalized advice.
- Avoid cannabis use during pregnancy, breastfeeding, and in individuals with a personal or family history of psychosis.
- Prefer products with lower THC concentrations to reduce psychoactive risks.
- Use non-smoking consumption methods such as vaporizers or edibles, but be cautious of dosage due to delayed onset.
- Do not drive or operate heavy machinery after cannabis use to prevent accidents.
- Be aware of cannabis laws and purchase products through legal, regulated sources where possible.
- Start with low doses and increase gradually to monitor individual tolerance and effects.
- Seek help if experiencing difficulty controlling cannabis use or negative impact on daily life.

## Safety Notes
- Cannabis can impair cognitive and motor functions; avoid tasks requiring full alertness post-use.
- Youth and adolescents are particularly vulnerable to adverse neurodevelopmental effects.
- Potential interactions exist between cannabis and prescribed medications metabolized by liver enzymes.
- Smoking cannabis carries respiratory risks similar to tobacco smoke.
- Mislabeling and contamination of unregulated cannabis products may increase harm.
- Pregnant or breastfeeding individuals should completely avoid cannabis use due to fetal and infant risks.

## Common Misconceptions
### Cannabis is completely safe and non-addictive because it is natural.
**Reality:** While cannabis is a plant, it can cause dependency and adverse effects, particularly with heavy or early use.

### All cannabis products produce the same effects.
**Reality:** Cannabis products vary widely in THC and CBD levels, affecting their psychoactive and therapeutic impacts.

### CBD causes intoxication or a 'high'.
**Reality:** CBD is non-intoxicating and does not produce a 'high' like THC.

## Clinical Pearls
- Educate patients on differences between THC and CBD to better understand therapeutic versus psychoactive effects.
- Assess mental health history before recommending or discussing cannabis use.
- Use validated screening tools for cannabis use disorder in routine clinical evaluation.
- Emphasize harm reduction methods especially in adolescent or vulnerable populations.
- Monitoring cannabis use should include consideration of potency, frequency, and method of ingestion.

## Glossary
- **Cannabis:** A genus of plants whose dried flowers and extracts are used recreationally and medicinally.
- **Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC):** The main psychoactive compound in cannabis responsible for the 'high' effect.
- **Cannabidiol (CBD):** A non-intoxicating cannabinoid studied for potential therapeutic effects such as reducing anxiety and inflammation.
- **Endocannabinoid System (ECS):** A biological system of receptors and endogenous cannabinoids that modulates various physiological processes.
- **Cannabinoid receptors:** Protein receptors (CB1 and CB2) in the body that bind cannabinoids affecting neurological and immune functions.
- **Cannabis use disorder (CUD):** A problematic pattern of cannabis use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress.
- **Bioavailability:** The proportion of a substance that enters circulation and is able to have an active effect.
- **Psychoactive:** Having a mental or behavioral effect on the brain, such as altering perception, mood, or consciousness.
